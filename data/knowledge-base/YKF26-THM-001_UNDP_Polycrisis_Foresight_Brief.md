POLYCRISIS AND LONG-TERM THINKING
REIMAGINING DEVELOPMENT IN ASIA AND THE PACIFIC FORESIGHT BRIEF | AUGUST 2022
UNDP Regional Bureau for Asia and the Pacific — Strategic Foresight Network
Authors: Caroline Baylon (Risk Management and Foresight Consultant), Sophia Robele (UNDP RBAP)
Citation: UNDP (2022). UNDP RBAP Foresight Brief: Polycrisis and Long-term Thinking. New York.

## INTRODUCTION
This foresight brief examines how the increased pace of globalization since the mid 20th century has brought a slew of new, growing risks — many existential, many interconnected — giving rise to cascading failures and shocks. Traditional risk-management frameworks are not equipped to deal with the complexity of existential and interconnected risks. Addressing them requires more long-term and cross-disciplinary thinking.

## THE ANTHROPOCENE AND NEW, GROWING RISKS
Humanity now lives in the Anthropocene Epoch, the first period in which human activity is the dominant influence on Earth's systems. Existential risks (nuclear weapons, unaligned AI, engineered pandemics) are now mostly man-made, unlike the natural risks (asteroid impact, supervolcano) humanity faced historically — and we have almost no track record of surviving them.

Interconnected/systemic risk has also risen sharply: feedback loops mean an impact on one element of a system reverberates onto others, amplifying effects (e.g. the climate change–forest fire feedback loop, or the melting Arctic sea ice feedback loop). Climate change acts as a "threat multiplier" cascading into pandemics, poverty, inequality and armed conflict.

Homer-Dixon et al. (2022) define the resulting condition as a "global polycrisis — a single, macro-crisis of interconnected, runaway failures of Earth's vital natural and social systems that irreversibly degrades humanity's prospects."

## LONG-TERM THINKING — WHAT INSTITUTIONS CAN DO
Four institutional levers are proposed:
1. **Embedding long-term thinking into political mandates and structures** — e.g. Wales' Future Generations Commissioner (Well-Being of Future Generations Act 2015); parliamentary future committees in Finland and Scotland; well-being budgets (New Zealand, 2019, tracking 12 indicators beyond GDP).
2. **Applying long-term and anticipatory thinking to planning and governance** — strategic foresight methods; anticipatory governance (case study: Singapore's Centre for Strategic Futures).
3. **Assessment methods** — future/intergenerational impact assessments; the UNDP RBAP Futures Impact Assessment (draft framework included as Appendix 1), central question: "Could this policy restrict future choices or opportunities for the policy's target population?"
4. **Supporting long-term thinking in the private sector** — discouraging short-term earnings guidance, linking executive pay to sustainability targets, alternative GDP-adjacent metrics.

## CROSS-DISCIPLINARY THINKING
Systemic risks require cross-disciplinary approaches because policy trade-offs in one domain (e.g. economic growth vs. emissions) ripple into others. The brief recommends "systemic design" (systems thinking + design practice + iterative experimentation) over systems thinking alone, and highlights the UNDP SDG Accelerator and Bottleneck Assessment Tool as a practical instrument for governments to move from system analysis to concrete policy entry points.

## CONSTRAINTS
Two open challenges are flagged: (1) how to meaningfully represent the interests of future generations in present-day decision-making, and (2) how to design governance for future *uncertainty*, not just future *risk* — including "unknown unknowns." Finland's "humble government" concept (policy-making that begins by acknowledging prevailing uncertainty) is cited as an emerging model.
